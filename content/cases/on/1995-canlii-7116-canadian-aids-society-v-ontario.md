# Canadian AIDS Society v. Ontario

## 30-second read

A historic Ontario challenge to mandatory reporting of HIV-positive blood-donor results. The court recognized substantial privacy and security-of-the-person interests but upheld the reporting regime because of the public-health purpose and the confidentiality protections surrounding public-health use of the information. The Ontario Court of Appeal affirmed the result in **1996 CanLII 1139 (ON CA)**, and leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada was refused.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

This is an important early Ontario authority for the proposition that public-health reporting can engage serious privacy interests without necessarily being unconstitutional. It is especially useful when thinking about the legal architecture behind mandatory reporting, case/contact management, and the constrained use of identifiable health information by public-health authorities.

## Factual background

Blood had been donated before HIV testing was available. Stored samples were later tested and a group of donors were identified as HIV-positive. The Canadian AIDS Society challenged the application of provincial reporting requirements that would result in positive results being communicated to public-health authorities and the affected donors, arguing in part that the donors had not consented to the later testing and that disclosure implicated constitutional privacy interests.

## Issues

1. Did the mandatory reporting of the HIV-positive results improperly interfere with security-of-the-person/privacy interests protected by s. 7 of the Charter?
2. Did the testing/reporting context amount to an unreasonable search or seizure under s. 8?
3. How should individual privacy interests be balanced against the state's public-health objective?

## Decision

The reporting requirements were upheld.

## Reasoning

The court treated the personal medical information as attracting a serious privacy interest, but concluded that the public-health objective and the safeguards surrounding public-health use of the information justified the reporting regime. The decision emphasized that the information was being transmitted into a statutory public-health system rather than disclosed indiscriminately.
