# Ontario (Attorney General) v. Health Services Appeal and Review Board

## 30-second read

The Northwestern Health Unit attempted to use HPPA s. 13 health-hazard orders to prohibit smoking across hospitality premises in the health unit. HSARB rescinded the orders and the Divisional Court upheld that result. Although s. 13 is broad, it could not be used as an effectively health-unit-wide, indefinite substitute for legislation of general application.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

This is a foundational Ontario case on the **limits of broad public-health order-making powers**. It is useful whenever an intervention is moving from a fact-specific response toward a rule that looks legislative in scope.

## Factual background

The MOH had advocated for broad indoor-smoking restrictions. In the absence of uniform provincial or municipal prohibitions, the health unit began issuing s. 13 orders to hospitality businesses where smoking occurred. Six businesses were treated as representative cases, but the MOH acknowledged that the initiative was intended to extend throughout the health unit. The orders were essentially identical apart from site-specific identifiers and observations.

HSARB did not decide whether second-hand smoke could constitute a health hazard. It instead concluded that the MOH had gone beyond the statutory authority conferred by s. 13. The Attorney General sought judicial review.

## Issues

1. Did s. 13 authorize the MOH/PHIs to issue the impugned smoking orders?
2. How should broad order-making language be interpreted in the context of delegated statutory power and the broader legislative scheme?

## Decision

The Divisional Court upheld HSARB.

## Reasoning

The court recognized that s. 13 and the statutory definition of health hazard are broad, but interpreted them in the context of the HPPA and Ontario's broader legislative framework. The court accepted HSARB's conclusion that the provision did not authorize an MOH to use serial individual orders to accomplish what was, in substance, a general and indefinite health-unit-wide smoking prohibition.

The case is therefore not authority that second-hand smoke cannot be a health hazard. Its central lesson is about **statutory fit and the character of the regulatory instrument being used**.
