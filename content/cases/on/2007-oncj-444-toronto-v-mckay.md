# Medical Officer of Health (City of Toronto) v. McKay

## 30-second read

Toronto's Associate Medical Officer of Health sought to extend the hospital detention of a person with extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB). The Ontario Court of Justice granted a further period of detention after considering both the severe consequences if McKay became infectious again and the likelihood that his history of treatment non-adherence and substance use would undermine a community-based plan. The case is a useful example of assessing future public risk and less-restrictive alternatives when seeking continued detention under the HPPA.

## Why this matters to MOHs/AMOHs

*McKay* is particularly useful because the court did more than identify the seriousness of XDR-TB. It considered the individual's history, the reasons for previous treatment non-adherence, proposed supports in the community, the likelihood of future adherence, and the consequences if treatment failed. For MOHs/AMOHs considering an exceptional measure such as continued detention, the decision illustrates the importance of connecting the statutory public-risk threshold to the person's actual circumstances and addressing whether a less-restrictive plan can adequately manage that risk.

## Factual background

McKay had extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis and had been detained in hospital under the HPPA. His drug resistance had developed in the context of previous non-adherence to tuberculosis treatment. The evidence also addressed longstanding alcohol use and other health and social circumstances that had affected his ability to adhere reliably to treatment.

The Associate Medical Officer of Health applied to extend McKay's detention for a further period. McKay was prepared to continue treatment but opposed continued hospital detention. He relied on positive changes in his circumstances, including support from a partner, as evidence that he could safely continue treatment in the community.

The public health position was that the risk could not be assessed solely on McKay's infectious status at the time of the hearing. If treatment adherence failed and his disease relapsed to an infectious state, the combination of XDR-TB and the limited treatment options could create a serious risk to the public.

## Issues

- Whether McKay's discharge from hospital would present a significant risk to the health of the public within the meaning of the HPPA.
- Whether his history and current circumstances supported confidence that treatment could be completed safely in the community.
- Whether proposed community supports were a sufficiently reliable alternative to continued detention.

## Decision / holding

The Ontario Court of Justice granted the application to extend McKay's detention.

The court was satisfied that discharge would present a significant risk to public health. In reaching that conclusion, it considered McKay's history of treatment non-adherence and substance use, the possibility of relapse or renewed infectiousness, and the serious consequences if XDR-TB were transmitted in the community.

## Reasoning

The court assessed risk prospectively rather than asking only whether McKay was infectious at the moment of the hearing. His previous treatment history and the factors that had contributed to non-adherence were relevant because failure to maintain treatment could result in renewed infectiousness.

The court also considered the proposed alternative of treatment in the community with support from McKay's partner. It concluded that the proposed arrangement did not provide sufficient assurance that the risks associated with future non-adherence could be adequately controlled.

The seriousness of the possible consequence was central to the analysis. Because McKay had XDR-TB, renewed infectiousness could expose others to a form of tuberculosis with very limited treatment options. The court therefore concluded that the statutory threshold of a significant risk to public health remained satisfied and that continued detention was justified.
