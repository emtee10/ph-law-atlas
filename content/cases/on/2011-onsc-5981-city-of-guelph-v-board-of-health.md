# City of Guelph v. Board of Health

## 30-second read

The City of Guelph challenged decisions of the Wellington-Dufferin-Guelph Board of Health concerning capital facilities and the City's resulting financial obligations. The Ontario Superior Court held that the HPPA gives a board of health authority to incur capital costs for facilities needed to carry out its functions, subject to the statutory requirement for consent from a majority of the municipalities in the health unit. An individual municipality does not have a unilateral veto over a board's capital decisions. The Court also considered a 1997 agreement among Guelph, Wellington and Dufferin, holding that a provision purporting to permit a municipality to withdraw from the Board of Health was invalid but that the remainder of the agreement continued to govern the allocation of financial responsibility among the municipalities.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

The case helps define the institutional relationship between a board of health and the municipalities that fund it. A board of health is a statutory body exercising authority under the HPPA rather than simply a joint committee of its constituent municipalities. Municipal involvement in major capital decisions is governed by the statutory framework: the consent requirement provides an important check, but does not give every municipality an individual veto.

The case is also relevant when public health units rely on agreements among obligated municipalities. Such agreements can address matters the HPPA allows municipalities to determine, including allocation of expenses, but cannot override or alter the statutory structure established by the Act.

## Factual background

The Wellington-Dufferin-Guelph Health Unit served the City of Guelph, the County of Wellington and the County of Dufferin. The three municipalities had entered into an agreement in 1997 addressing their relationship with the Health Unit, including the allocation of expenses.

The Board of Health undertook plans involving capital facilities for the Health Unit. The resulting capital expenditures would be shared by the obligated municipalities. Guelph disputed aspects of the Board's authority and the City's resulting financial obligations and brought an application against the Board of Health and the Counties of Wellington and Dufferin.

The dispute required the Court to consider both the statutory powers and financing structure created by the HPPA and the effect of the municipalities' 1997 agreement.

## Issues

The Court considered issues including:

1. the authority of a board of health under the HPPA to incur capital costs and acquire or hold property for the purposes of carrying out its functions;
2. the nature and extent of the municipal consent required for such capital decisions;
3. the extent to which an individual municipality could prevent a Board of Health decision supported by the required majority of constituent municipalities;
4. the obligation of municipalities to fund Board of Health expenses; and
5. the continuing validity and effect of the 1997 agreement among Guelph, Wellington and Dufferin, including a provision purporting to permit a municipality to withdraw from the Board of Health.

## Decision

The Court interpreted s.52 of the HPPA as permitting a board of health to incur capital costs associated with facilities, furniture and equipment required to carry out its public-health functions, subject to obtaining the consent of the councils of a majority of the municipalities served by the board.

The statutory consent requirement did not give each municipality an independent veto over the Board's decision. For a health unit composed of three obligated municipalities, consent from two municipalities could satisfy the majority requirement.

The Court also held that the provision of the 1997 municipal agreement purporting to allow an individual municipality to withdraw from the Board of Health was invalid because the composition and existence of the Board were governed by statute. The invalidity of that provision did not invalidate the agreement as a whole. The agreement continued to operate as the basis for allocating financial responsibility among the member municipalities for Health Unit expenses.

## Reasoning

The Court approached the Board of Health as a statutory entity whose powers and responsibilities arise from the HPPA. Although municipalities appoint members and are responsible for substantial portions of a health unit's expenses, the Board exercises its own statutory functions rather than acting merely as an agent of the municipalities.

Section 52 gives boards of health authority in relation to property needed to carry out their functions. The Court viewed the requirement for majority municipal consent as a mechanism for ensuring that capital expenditures serve legitimate Health Unit purposes rather than extraneous purposes. It did not interpret the requirement as conferring a veto on every constituent municipality.

The financing provisions of the HPPA similarly require obligated municipalities to pay the expenses incurred by or on behalf of the Board of Health in carrying out its statutory functions. The municipalities may agree among themselves on how those expenses are apportioned.

Against that statutory framework, the municipalities could not use a private agreement to create a right to withdraw from a Board of Health where the HPPA did not provide such a right. That provision was therefore ineffective. However, it was severable from the balance of the agreement, which continued to govern the municipalities' respective shares of Health Unit expenses.
