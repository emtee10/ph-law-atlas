# Regional Municipality of Halton v. Patrick Bowman

## 30-second read

A barber-shop operator was convicted of obstructing a public health inspector who was attempting to complete a routine inspection. The Ontario Court of Justice treated the offence as a strict-liability regulatory offence: the prosecution had to prove the prohibited conduct, after which the accused could rely on due diligence or an honest and reasonable mistake of fact.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

Most public-health legal libraries overemphasize major Charter cases. This case answers a much more routine operational question: **what does obstruction of a PHI look like in practice, and what evidence should staff capture when it occurs?**

## Factual background

A PHI attended a barber shop for an annual inspection. A disagreement developed after the inspector raised cleanliness concerns about disinfectant solution. The operator became angry and, through his words and conduct, prevented the inspector from completing the inspection. The prosecution alleged obstruction contrary to HPPA s. 42.

## Issues

1. Did the defendant hinder or obstruct a PHI who was lawfully carrying out statutory duties?
2. What mental element applies to the offence?
3. Was a due-diligence or reasonable-mistake defence established?

## Decision

The court found the prohibited conduct proven and convicted.

## Reasoning

The court characterized the HPPA as public-welfare legislation and treated the offence as one of strict liability. Once the actus reus was established beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant could avoid liability by proving reasonable care or an honest and reasonable mistake of fact on a balance of probabilities. He did not do so.

The decision treats obstruction functionally: conduct that prevents or materially interferes with completion of a lawful inspection can satisfy the provision even without physical violence.
