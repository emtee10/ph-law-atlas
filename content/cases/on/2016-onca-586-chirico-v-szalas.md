# Chirico v. Szalas

## 30-second read

After a dog had bitten several people, an s. 13 health-hazard order ultimately required that the dog be surrendered for euthanasia. The owner sent the dog outside Canada and did not comply. The Court of Appeal held that the contempt proceeding was properly before it and found contempt, returning the matter for the appropriate purge/sanction process. The appellate decision is most useful as an **enforcement/compliance** case, not as a fresh merits ruling on whether the dog was a health hazard.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

The HPPA is often used in situations where compliance is more important than punishment. *Chirico* illustrates the interaction between a final public-health order and the court's contempt jurisdiction when a respondent deliberately acts to defeat the order.

## Factual background

The dog had been involved in multiple unprovoked bites. A public-health order under s. 13 required action culminating in surrender of the dog for euthanasia. There had been proceedings before HSARB and the courts concerning the order. Rather than surrender the dog, the respondent arranged for it to be moved to the United States.

## Issues

The Court of Appeal was primarily concerned with the contempt proceeding: whether the disposition below was final/interlocutory for appeal purposes and whether the respondent's conduct amounted to contempt of the operative court/order framework.

## Decision 

The appeal was allowed.

# Reasoning

The Court of Appeal concluded that the contempt matter was properly appealable in the circumstances and that the respondent was in contempt. The operative obligation was sufficiently clear, and deliberately arranging circumstances so that literal compliance became impossible did not defeat enforcement.
