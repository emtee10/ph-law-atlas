# Ontario Nurses' Assn. v. Chief Medical Officer of Health (Ontario)

## 30-second read

The Ontario Nurses' Association challenged CMOH Directives #1 and #5, arguing that they inadequately addressed aerosol/asymptomatic COVID-19 transmission and N95 use. The Divisional Court dismissed the application. The directives were reasonable because they incorporated the precautionary principle, reflected the evidence and context before the CMOH, and allowed health professionals to use N95 respirators based on point-of-care risk assessment and professional judgment.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

Although this concerns the provincial CMOH rather than a local MOH order, it is valuable authority on **reasonableness review of public-health decisions under evolving scientific conditions**.

## Factual background

The applicants sought judicial review of directives governing infection prevention/control and PPE in hospitals, long-term care and other health-care contexts. They argued the directives should more broadly mandate N95 respirators in light of evidence regarding aerosol and asymptomatic transmission.

## Issues

1. What standard applies to review of CMOH directives?
2. Were the directives reasonable in light of the scientific evidence and the statutory precautionary principle?
3. Did the directives infringe s. 7 of the Charter?

## Decision

The application was dismissed. 

## Reasoning

The court characterized the directives as quasi-legislative decisions and applied reasonableness review. It found that the precautionary principle was expressly built into the directives and that the PPE framework permitted access to N95 respirators where professional judgment/point-of-care assessment supported their use.

Importantly, the court recognized that the reasonableness of an ongoing directive can be affected by changing facts and evidence. A measure that was reasonable when made is not automatically reasonable forever.
