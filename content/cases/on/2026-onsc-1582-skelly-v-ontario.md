# Skelly v. Ontario

## 30-second read

The operator associated with Adamson Barbecue challenged Ontario pandemic measures and Toronto's response to his deliberate reopening for indoor dining. The Superior Court dismissed the judicial-review and Charter claims. It held that the Toronto MOH acted within HPPA authority in issuing an s. 22 order and s. 24 enforcement directions, and emphasized the **staged** nature of the response when assessing proportionality.

## Why this matters to an MOH/AMOH

This recent case is useful for thinking about the full arc from investigation to order to escalated enforcement, particularly where state action is later challenged as disproportionate or Charter-infringing.

## Factual background

In November 2020 the applicant publicly announced that he would reopen a Toronto restaurant for indoor dining contrary to then-applicable pandemic restrictions. Toronto received complaints and public health officials investigated. The MOH issued an s. 22 order. When the applicant continued to defy the restrictions/order, the MOH issued enforcement directions under s. 24 and Toronto took further steps to secure the premises. The applicant later brought constitutional, Charter and judicial-review claims.

## Issues

Among a larger set of constitutional questions, the court considered:

- whether the MOH had statutory authority for the s. 22 order and s. 24 directions;
- whether the MOH's actions were reasonable on judicial review; and
- whether those actions infringed Charter rights or, if so, were justified.

## Decision

The application was dismissed. 

## Reasoning

The court concluded that the MOH acted within statutory authority and that the measures were reasonable. In its proportionality analysis, the court emphasized that the response was staged: the MOH began with an order that still allowed lawful forms of food sales and escalated after the applicant announced and demonstrated that he would not comply. The public health benefits were found to outweigh the effects on the business.
